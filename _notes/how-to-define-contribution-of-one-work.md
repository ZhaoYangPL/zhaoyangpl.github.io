*Reflections on Professor Huan Wang's lecture at Westlake University*

One of the most thought-provoking questions I took away from Professor Huan Wang's lecture was deceptively simple: **What truly constitutes a research contribution, and how do we cultivate good research taste?**

We often evaluate research by what it achieves: a more accurate model, a faster algorithm, or a new state-of-the-art result. Yet these achievements do not necessarily tell us how much a piece of work contributes to our understanding of the world.

A useful way to think about research contributions is through three interconnected dimensions: **theory, modeling, and methods or implementation.**

Theory seeks to explain *why* something works, revealing fundamental principles that may remain valid long after particular technologies become obsolete. Modeling asks a different but equally important question: *What is the right way to understand and formulate the problem?* By identifying essential structures and making assumptions explicit, a good model can transform a seemingly complicated problem into one that can be systematically studied. Methods and implementation, meanwhile, address *how* to solve the problem effectively, often creating substantial practical and industrial value.

These dimensions are not necessarily ranked by importance. A methodological breakthrough can open an entirely new research direction, just as an elegant theory may have little immediate practical impact. What matters is not merely the category of a contribution, but the depth of insight it brings.

This distinction made me reflect on the meaning of **research taste**.

It is relatively easy to identify something that could be improved. Almost every existing system has limitations, every benchmark has room for improvement, and every algorithm offers opportunities for optimization. But the existence of an improvement opportunity does not automatically make it an important research question.

The harder task is to recognize which problems are worth our intellectual effort.

Does the problem expose a fundamental limitation in how we currently understand a system? Does solving it reveal a principle that generalizes beyond a particular implementation? Would the insight still matter if today's models, architectures, and benchmarks were replaced tomorrow?

These questions suggest that good research taste involves more than technical competence. It requires the ability to distinguish **what is merely improvable from what is genuinely worth understanding**.

This is particularly relevant to my current research on adaptive context management for long-horizon LLM agents. Developing a more cost-efficient compaction algorithm would be a valuable methodological contribution. But understanding the underlying trade-offs between information retention, computational cost, and decision-making under uncertainty could lead to something more general: a framework for reasoning about resource allocation in intelligent systems.

Perhaps the most important lesson is that **the quality of research is not determined solely by how well we solve a problem, but also by the significance of the problem we choose to solve.**

Ultimately, research taste is not just the ability to recognize a promising solution. It is the judgment to see a meaningful question where others see only an engineering challenge—and the curiosity to pursue an explanation rather than settle for an improvement.

**A better method may improve a system. A better question may change how we think about the system altogether.**
